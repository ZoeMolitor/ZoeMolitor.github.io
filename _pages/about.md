Hi, I'm Dr. Zoe Molitor, a structural petrologist and Postdoctoral Fellow at Boston College. I am currently part of Juliet Ryan-Davis' research group and actively studying the petrogenesis of Appalachian leucogranites (see her website [here](https://www.julietryandavis.com/)).

My research addresses how heat, melt, and fluids shape the mechanical evolution of Earth's lithosphere — from the thermal structure and rheology of individual mountain belts to the processes that drive plate boundaries to reorganize over hundreds of millions of years. Much of what we know about the deep lithosphere comes from indirect methods like seismic or gravity surveys, or from experiments whose results are rarely tested against real rock. My work combines field-based structural and petrographic analysis, geochronology, and microanalytical and thermodynamic modeling to build direct, empirical constraints on how the deep crust actually behaves.
This work matters beyond questions of mountain-building: the structure and evolution of the deep lithosphere is a first-order control on how elements are distributed and concentrated in the crust, directly shaping where and how critical mineral resources form. Further, the rheology of Earth's crust controls the occurence and spatial distribution of earthquakes and volcanic eruptions, which pose significant natural hazards to population centers.

I began my studies as a computer science student at Purdue University; but alas, a desk-heavy job was not for me. Drawing on my childhood obsession with rock collecting, I brought my computational aptitude into the Geology and Geophysics department at Purdue University where I worked on several research projects with Profs. Chris Andronicos (structural geology and petrology), Julie Elliott (geodesy), and Ken Ridgway (stratigraphy and structure). These projects jump-started my interest in research and structural geology. In 2024, I completed my Ph.D. at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology, supervised by Professor Oliver Jagoutz. It was during my Ph.D. that I realized the potential for deep crustal, process-oriented research in the Appalachian mountain belt of the eastern United States. In summer 2024, I was awarded an NSF EAR Postdoctoral Fellowship to study the thermal evolution and rheology of the southern New England Appalachians. Together with my mentor, Professor Jay Ague, I created a large dataset of metamorphic pressure-temperature-time data spanning several lithotectonic units. This research sparked my interest in integrating the metamorphic and leucogranite records in southern and central New England in greater detail. The New England Appalachians are shaping up to be an excellent analogue and natural laboratory for the modern-day Tibetan plateau, but with significantly more exposure of the middle and lower crust.

Outside of research, well, I still love to look at rocks. No one who comes on vacation with me is safe from my geologic tidbits and analysis. I love to hike, camp, and backpack, particularly around the northeast USA. I am typically accompanied by my canine companion, Reddy, an exhuberant Australian Cattle Dog, who one day will be able to sniff out garnets for me. I'm also engaged within my local community as much as possible, hosting K12 students at workshops or visiting classrooms to describe my research and the flexibility of a career in Earth science. At home I occasionally play video games, love to cook (but not clean...), and of course play with Reddy.
